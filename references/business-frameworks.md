# Business frameworks: choose by the decision

These five recipes complement the [tree methods](tree-methods.md). They are not additional tree shapes, a universal sequence, or five exercises every case must complete. Sources were checked on **10 September 2026**. Every example below is original and fictional: its stated facts are exercise inputs, not research findings or the authors' cases.

## Use with the conversational agent

Recover the decision, business boundary, time horizon and evidence first. Explain why one framework helps. If its required inputs are missing, name the gaps or choose another approach; do not invent market shares, competitor behavior or customer demand to fill a template.

Preserve the framework's actual representation: a forces analysis, a pairing matrix, a linked policy argument, an integrated cascade or a portfolio matrix. Discuss it in the agent conversation and record its findings and supporting references using the current [schema](schema.md). Use a linked analysis document when the supported contract permits one; otherwise retain the complete explanation in notes or a clearly identified companion document. Do not invent schema fields or a `method` value named after a business framework. Only put derived questions, hypotheses, options and evidence into the tree where their relationships are meaningful.

These are agent instructions and examples. This library does not by itself add dedicated framework editors, market data, automatic strategic validation or certified recommendations. Keep supplied facts, analytical inferences and proposed choices distinct. A framework produces a more inspectable judgment; it does not establish its premises.

| Decision need | Useful framework | Expected result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understand an industry's profit pressures and possible positions | Five Forces | Explained structural pressures and positioning implications |
| Turn an internal/external assessment into candidate moves | SWOT followed by TOWS | Options tied to specific factor pairings |
| Design a focused response to a consequential challenge | Rumelt's kernel | Diagnosis, guiding policy and mutually supporting actions |
| Choose how a business will compete | Playing to Win | Five reinforcing choices and their critical assumptions |
| Allocate resources across existing businesses | BCG growth-share | Portfolio positions and investment questions to investigate |

## Porter: Five Forces

**Scope and source.** Michael Porter's framework examines industry structure and how competitive pressures affect the distribution of economic value. It informs industry attractiveness and strategic positioning; a firm's resources and relative position still matter. Primary sources: [Harvard Business School's Institute for Strategy and Competitiveness: Five Forces](https://www.isc.hbs.edu/strategy/business-strategy/Pages/the-five-forces.aspx) and [business strategy](https://www.isc.hbs.edu/strategy/business-strategy/Pages/default.aspx).

**Mechanism.** Bound the industry, geography, customers and period. Examine rivalry, entry barriers, supplier power, buyer power and substitutes. For each, connect observable conditions to prices, costs or investment requirements. A substitute meets the same need through a different approach; another provider of the same service belongs under rivalry. Assess interactions and likely change, then identify positions that reduce exposure or alter the pressures. Do not average five unsupported scores into an attractiveness verdict.

**Worked fictional example.** A laundry operator considers serving hotels in one region over the next three years. Stipulated case inputs:

| Force | Case input | Inference to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rivalry | Three laundries have unused plant capacity and bid for the same contracts. | Discounting to fill capacity may compress margins. |
| Buyer power | Two hotel groups purchase most outsourced laundry in the region. | Losing a large contract is costly; buyers may extract concessions. |
| Supplier power | Only two local firms maintain the installed industrial machines. | Maintenance terms and downtime may raise operating costs. |
| Entry | A viable operation needs a plant and dense collection routes. | Capital and route-building requirements may deter entrants. |
| Substitutes | Some hotels retain usable on-site laundry rooms. | In-house operation may cap the price hotels will accept. |

The initial implication is to investigate whether smaller independent hotels value a dependable collection service enough to support attractive economics. It is not yet a recommendation: willingness to pay, route costs and incumbent responses remain unknown.

**Limit / unsuitable case.** This does not establish a particular entrant's profitability or forecast competitor behavior. It is unsuitable for deciding which five administrative tasks to finish today; those tasks are not an industry with bargaining relationships.

## SWOT and Weihrich's TOWS matrix

**Scope and source.** SWOT organizes relevant internal strengths/weaknesses and external opportunities/threats. TOWS makes the additional move of systematically matching those factors to generate strategic alternatives. Attribute TOWS to **Heinz Weihrich, “The TOWS Matrix—A Tool for Situational Analysis,” Long Range Planning 15(2), 54–66 (1982)**. This guide makes no claim about a sole inventor of SWOT. Primary paper: [university-hosted original article, especially pp. 59–61](https://elearning15.unibg.it/pluginfile.php/644921/mod_folder/content/0/STR%20T06%20Weihrich_1982_LRP.pdf); [publisher DOI record](https://doi.org/10.1016/0024-6301(82)90120-0).

**Mechanism.** Fix the business objective and horizon; substantiate and identify each factor. Pair strengths with opportunities (**SO**), weaknesses with opportunities (**WO**), strengths with threats (**ST**) and weaknesses with threats (**WT**). Explain how each pairing suggests an action. Evaluate feasibility, risk, timing and consistency before selecting a combination. The four cells can overlap; they are not an exhaustive partition or four mandatory initiatives. Update factors as conditions change.

**Worked fictional example.** A bike-repair shop considers its next-year service mix. Its exercise inputs are **S1:** skilled diagnostic mechanics trusted by local commuters; **W1:** manual booking limits scheduling capacity; **O1:** a new office development has requested proposals for scheduled on-site bike repairs; **T1:** a national chain will open nearby with discounted walk-in servicing.

| Pairing | Candidate move | Why this follows |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SO: S1 + O1 | Propose an on-site diagnostic and repair service for the office development. | Existing specialist skill could meet the requested service need. |
| WO: W1 + O1 | Develop consolidated appointment booking with the office manager before bidding. | The opportunity is usable only if scheduling can scale. |
| ST: S1 + T1 | Emphasize complex fault diagnosis with clearly scoped pricing. | Distinctive expertise could reduce direct competition on routine-service discounts. |
| WT: W1 + T1 | Avoid high-volume discount promotions while repairing the booking bottleneck. | It limits exposure to a price contest the current operation cannot serve reliably. |

These are alternatives to compare, not four approved projects. The office contract's economics, the value of specialist diagnosis and the required scheduling investment still need evidence.

**Limit / unsuitable case.** A list of positive and negative adjectives is not a TOWS analysis. The matrix does not calculate break-even attendance for a priced workshop; use a model for that numerical question.

## Richard Rumelt: the kernel of good strategy

**Scope and source.** The kernel describes the structure of a coherent response to a challenge in **Richard Rumelt's Good Strategy/Bad Strategy (2011)**. It is not the entirety of the book's strategic reasoning. Primary sources: [Rumelt's explanation in McKinsey Quarterly](https://www.mckinsey.com/capabilities/strategy-and-corporate-finance/our-insights/the-perils-of-bad-strategy) and [publisher's book record](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/208668/good-strategy-bad-strategy-by-richard-rumelt/).

**Mechanism.** Develop a **diagnosis** that identifies the critical nature of the obstacle. Choose a **guiding policy** for addressing it. Design **coherent actions** that reinforce one another and implement that policy with available resources. Explain why the diagnosis supports the policy and why the actions work together; identify what must be forgone. Revise when evidence undermines the diagnosis. Ambitious targets and a longer task list do not substitute for this argument.

**Worked fictional example.** A small invoicing-software company has stalled after signing new customers. A stipulated review finds eight of ten recent accounts could not import their historical invoices without engineering help; support interviews describe the same barrier. Rival explanations, including poor overall product fit, remain possible.

| Element | Proposed strategy |
| --- | --- |
| Diagnosis | The current customer segment repeatedly stalls at importing history; onboarding capacity is a plausible growth constraint. |
| Guiding policy | Concentrate the next product cycle on dependable onboarding for this segment before entering adjacent segments. |
| Coherent actions | Standardize supported import formats; give one team ownership of import failures; postpone two adjacent-segment integrations; review time to first successful invoice and unresolved import failures weekly. |

The engineering focus, ownership and segment restraint reinforce the same policy. A disconfirming finding—successful imports without subsequent use—would require reopening the diagnosis rather than declaring the policy successful.

**Limit / unsuitable case.** The three headings do not verify causality, feasibility or execution. They are unsuitable as a mechanical ranking of an already specified daily task list; first determine whether there is a strategic obstacle requiring a coordinated response.

## Lafley and Martin: Playing to Win choice cascade

**Scope and source.** **A.G. Lafley and Roger L. Martin, Playing to Win: How Strategy Really Works (2013)** addresses the integrated choices by which an organization seeks to win. Primary sources: [Martin's Strategy page](https://rogerlmartin.com/thought-pillars/strategy) and [his explanation of integration failures](https://rogermartin.medium.com/overcoming-the-integrative-strategy-challenge-9af0d670d083).

**Mechanism.** Connect a winning aspiration, where to play, how to win there, required capabilities and the management systems that sustain those capabilities. Work back and forth: a where-to-play choice without a credible how-to-win is incomplete; missing capabilities may require revising both. Make excluded choices explicit. Identify the critical customer, competitive and delivery assumptions, then seek evidence that could invalidate them. This is an integrated cascade, not a one-way checklist or a complete testing process on its own.

**Worked fictional example.** A small accounting-software business is choosing a position. The following is a proposed set of choices, with no claimed market validation:

| Choice | Proposed answer |
| --- | --- |
| Winning aspiration | Become the accounting tool independent café owners choose because closing their books takes less effort. |
| Where to play | Single-site cafés in one country using either of two specified point-of-sale systems; reach them through specialist bookkeepers. |
| How to win | Deliver more dependable reconciliation of those systems with less owner intervention than generic bookkeeping software. |
| Capabilities | Maintain the two integrations, resolve café-specific reconciliation exceptions, and onboard bookkeepers effectively. |
| Management systems | Review unmatched transactions and service effort per account; fund integration maintenance; require an explicit strategy review before accepting unsupported point-of-sale systems. |

Serving every small business would expand the integration and exception burden and may defeat the proposed advantage. Interview bookkeepers and examine actual reconciliation work before claiming that the focus is valued or economically defensible.

**Limit / unsuitable case.** A filled cascade does not prove customers will choose the offer. It is unsuitable for generating next quarter's revenue target alone: a target omits the choices and competitive logic the framework requires.

## BCG: growth-share portfolio matrix

**Scope and source.** BCG's framework compares businesses or product businesses within a portfolio to inform resource allocation. **Bruce Henderson popularized it in “The Product Portfolio” (1970)**; BCG describes its development as collaborative. Primary sources: [Henderson's original essay](https://www.bcg.com/publications/1970/strategy-the-product-portfolio), [BCG's framework overview](https://www.bcg.com/about/overview/our-history/growth-share-matrix) and [BCG's 2014 reassessment](https://www.bcg.com/publications/2014/growth-share-matrix-bcg-classics-revisited).

**Mechanism.** Define each served market and comparable measurement period. Plot **market growth** against **relative market share**, documenting the share denominator and growth boundary. The combinations are stars (high/high), cash cows (low growth/high share), question marks (high growth/low share) and dogs/pets (low/low). Investigate cash generation, investment needed to sustain or gain position, and alternative uses of capital. The original logic links leadership to cost advantage and growth to cash requirements; test whether those assumptions hold in this portfolio.

**Worked fictional example.** A software group reviews four businesses. All inputs are stipulated, for defined markets over the same year. For this example only, relative share is our share divided by the largest external competitor's share; **1.0** separates leading/nonleading positions and **8% market growth** separates high/low growth. Those thresholds are exercise assumptions, not universal BCG rules.

| Business | Market growth | Our / competitor share | Relative share | Position |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Payroll | 3% | 30% / 20% | 1.5 | Cash cow |
| Inventory | 14% | 24% / 16% | 1.5 | Star |
| Scheduling | 18% | 6% / 30% | 0.2 | Question mark |
| Legacy fax | −4% | 4% / 20% | 0.2 | Dog/pet |

Investigate whether payroll produces surplus cash after necessary maintenance, whether inventory can profitably defend its position, and whether scheduling has an affordable route to advantage. Review the fax unit's cash flows and dependencies before considering repositioning or exit. Quadrant placement alone does not approve a transfer of funds or a divestment.

**Limit / unsuitable case.** Market boundaries can change the result; share and growth are proxies, not intrinsic value. BCG's reassessment questions the continuing strength of the share/competitiveness link. This is unsuitable for prioritizing pre-revenue ideas without meaningful market-share data, or for labeling personal activities as stars and dogs.

## Review an application

Before presenting a result, check the question fit, required inputs, original mechanism, evidence/inference boundary, consequences and limitations. Include at least one plausible challenge to the proposed implication. Preserve missing information explicitly and link any next analysis to the decision it could change. Framework coverage means a useful, checked application—not a filled grid or a count of names.
